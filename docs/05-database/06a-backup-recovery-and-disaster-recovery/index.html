Backup, Recovery, and Disaster Recovery

Study prompt: Learn full, incremental, and logical backups; restore drills; point-in-time recovery; RPO and RTO; replication versus backup; regional failures; and how to document and test a disaster-recovery runbook.

Backup Types

TypeWhat it captures
FullA complete copy of the database at one point in time — simplest to restore, largest and slowest to take.
IncrementalOnly what changed since the last backup (of any type) — smaller and faster, but restoring means replaying a chain of them.
LogicalA dump of schema and data as SQL/exportable statements (e.g. pg_dump) — portable across versions, slower to restore on large datasets.
Continuous / WAL archivingShips the write-ahead log continuously, enabling point-in-time recovery to any moment, not just backup checkpoints.

RPO and RTO

  • RPO (Recovery Point Objective): how much data you can afford to lose, measured in time — "RPO of 5 minutes" means at most 5 minutes of writes are lost in a disaster.
  • RTO (Recovery Time Objective): how long you can afford to be down while recovering.
  • Backup frequency and method are chosen to hit the RPO; restore tooling and automation are chosen to hit the RTO — they're separate numbers with separate costs.

Replication Is Not a Backup

A replica copies changes in near-real time, including bad ones: a mistaken DELETE or a corrupted write replicates to every replica within seconds. Replication protects against a node failing; only a backup (plus point-in-time recovery) protects against a mistake or corruption.

Untested backups aren't backups

A backup that has never been restored is unverified — encoding bugs, missing permissions, or silent truncation only surface at restore time, which is the worst possible moment to discover them. Schedule regular restore drills.

Disaster Recovery Runbook

  • Document exact restore steps, who owns the decision to fail over, and how to verify data integrity afterward.
  • Plan for regional failures, not just single-node ones, if the business can't tolerate a full-region outage.
  • Rehearse the runbook on a schedule — a plan nobody has executed is a guess.
